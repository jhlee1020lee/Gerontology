at the Whistle Stop Café (Flagg, 1988), and
the 1991 movie version, Fried Green Tomatoes ,
reveal the value and meaning of friendship.
Intertwined stories follow Evelyn and Ninny,
middle-aged and older women in the 1980s
developing a close relationship after meeting
by chance, with flashbacks to the story of Idgie
and Ruth, two young adult women in the 1920s
looking after and caring about one

ionship, emotional support, solace,
practical assistance, loneliness, and
health concerns run through the
accounts of both pairs of friends and the larger
social networks in which they are embedded.
These are the same themes researchers have
studied while documenting the roles of friends
in contemporary adult life.
What Is Friendship?

For many, the definition of “friend” may be taken
for granted, yet friendship researchers recognize
the existence of many types of friends. They are
careful to differentiate across types (based on
perceived emotional closeness) and to distin-
guish feelings and activities relevant to the dif-
ferent types. When people are asked to define

friendship, they usually refer to a voluntary tie
involving affection, companionship, trust, and
reciprocity. They readily understand the mean-
ing of terms such as best friend, close friend, and
casual friend, and can easily describe similarities
and differences across such friend categories
(Adams and Blieszner, 1994; Adams, Blieszner,
and de Vries, 2000; Blieszner and Adams, 1992).
Note, however, that although friend relation-

ships are voluntary compared to most family
bonds, certain restrictions on friendship still
hold: people can only befriend people they meet,
and even voluntary relationships are subject to
social norms (Argyle and Henderson, 1984;
Felmlee and Muraco, 2009; Hall, 2011). Some
norms, such as reciprocity of self-disclosure and
of initiating contact, or mutuality of respect and
trust, are reflected in the trust and support
elements of the definition of friendship. Other

Unique Features of Friend Ties

Many family relationships belong to certain
segments of the lifespan and depend upon the
actions of others. Prepubescent children cannot
be parents, intergenerational bonds are contin-
gent upon availability of younger and older
family members, and becoming a grandparent
occurs not when one chooses, but when one’s
offspring bear children. In later years, retire-
ment and outliving a child or spouse remove
employee and parent or marital roles, respec-
tively. But friendship is a close relationship that
begins early and can endure throughout life.
The durability of friendship occurs both
because adults sustain longstanding friend ties
and because they make new ones. Matthews
(2000) identified several distinct approaches to
making new friends. Some people have a few
longstanding, close friendships and believe it
would not be possible to replace such friends if
they were lost to relocation, illness, or death.
Others are more likely to find new friends as life
circumstances change, but to be less intimate
with them. Still others acquire new friends
throughout life in an ever-expanding web of
close bonds. Unlike family relationships that
occur through the happenstance of birth or
marriage, friend circles resulting from these
friendship styles are more influenced by person-
ality, outlook, and choice.
Because friends tend to be age peers, they are
more likely than some family members to share
attitudes, values, and interests; such perceived
commonalities reinforce the development and
endurance of friendships. Although friends
might not agree all the time, the voluntary

nature of friendship means that positive inter-
actions are likely to outweigh negative ones in
ongoing friendships. Companionship and mutu-
al regard are high, and affectionate caring is a
dominant feature of the relationship. Unlike the
high rate of marital termination through divorce,
few people end friendships intentionally, and
only due to very serious betrayal or breach of
trust (Adams and Blieszner, 1998; Blieszner and
Adams, 1998). Most adults report having numer-
ous long-term friends as well as making new
ones, suggesting the continued meaningfulness
of this relationship across the years (Adams and
Blieszner, 1995; Blieszner, 1995).

playmates, observing and imitating each other as
they explore the world together. Preschoolers
learn personal initiative while balancing the
needs and wants of their friends, but friendships
may change frequently. School-age children
pursue longer-lasting friendships that supply
emotional support and interpersonal validation
as they master expectations for learning and
productivity. In adolescence, friendships deepen
and friend networks expand and receive more
attention as teens become increasingly indepen-
dent of their parents (Blieszner and Roberto,
2004). Adolescent friends can be powerful role
models—for good or ill (Piehler, Véronneau, and
Dishion, 2012).
Young adults develop the capacity for com-
mitment to lasting friendships as well as roman-
tic relationships. Friends serve as resources and
guides for meeting the many new challenges that
appear at this stage. Busy middle-aged adults
complain of having too little time for friends, yet
they manage to sustain and treasure the most
important friendships as they confront together
the challenges associated with tending family,
work, and community needs. Retirees are likely
to have more time for keeping in touch with
long-term friends who may live at a distance, as
well as making new ones for readily available
companionship and emotional support. Many
older adults thoughtfully choose to develop or
sustain those friendships that are most meaning-
ful, while letting others dwindle (Blieszner and
Roberto, 2004, 2012; Carstensen, 2006).
Friendships themselves proceed through
distinct phases, starting from the tentative
period of acquaintance when potential friends
first meet, then moving to the formation stage
when they arrange continued interaction and
opportunities to get to know one another bet-
ter. During the sustainment phase, emotional
closeness may deepen, remain stable, or lessen.
Key influences on friendship endurance are per-
ceived similarity on important attitudes and
values, shared interests or a willingness to be-
come involved in the friend’s pursuits, com-

fortable amounts and types of self-disclosure,
intentional efforts to sustain the friendship,
feelings of trust and mutual caring, expressions
of social and emotional support, and effective
communication and problem-solving strategies
for resolving disagreements.
Friendships may diminish in closeness or

end completely for a variety of reasons, includ-
ing changing interests, relocation without
sufficient effort to keep in touch, unresolved
conflicts, and betrayal. A friend who becomes
seriously ill may be unable to sustain the mutual-
ity and reciprocity deemed essential to friend-
ship, and people near the end of life may choose
to release friendships in favor of focusing limited
emotional energy on close family members
(Blieszner and Adams, 1992; Carstensen, 2006).
As research has shown, certain aspects of
friend interactions change with developmental
stages of life and evolving phases of closeness.
Nevertheless, the core elements of friendship—
companionship, mutual support, reciprocity,
affection—remain consistently stable and
important throughout life. Research on friend-
ship among nationalities other than North
Americans yields similar findings on the value of
friendship well into the later years (among the
Dutch: Stevens, Martina, and Westerhof, 2006;
Germans: Huxhold, Miche, and Schüz, 2013;
Hong Kong Chinese: Siu and Phillips, 2002;
Koreans: Park et al., 2013; Swedes: McCamish-
Svensson et al., 1999; and the Welsh: Jerrome
and Wenger, 1999).

Huxhold, Miche, and Schüz, 2013), probably
because the sense of obligation felt so strongly
within family relationships is absent from
friendships. Adults are much more likely to rely
upon friends than on relatives when it comes to
confiding important feelings or seeking solace
(Blieszner, 2001), probably because friends tend
to be age peers and most people feel comfortable
being intimate with those who are similar to
themselves rather than with others who may
have different life experiences (Gonzaga, 2009).
Friends assist each other with instrumental
help such as, in the later years of life, picking up
food at the grocery store, providing transporta-
tion to medical care, or sharing meals. They also
help foster good health by providing information
and emotional support, and by helping each
other comply with health and medication
regimens. They might offer advice about diet
and exercise, recommend medical practitioners,
listen to each other’s needs, enjoy each other’s
company as they engage in meaningful activities,
and laugh together (Gallant, Spitze, and Prohas-
ka, 2007; Moreman, 2008; Resnick et al., 2002).
By being involved in one another’s lives and
showing that they care, by “just
being there,” friends can
mitigate loneliness and depres-
sion (Green et al., 2001; Raw-
lins, 1994), especially in the face
of late-life conditions such as
chronic health problems or pain
(Roberto and Husser, 2007).
In contrast, as with any other close relation-
ship, friendships can be problematic and poten-
tially interfere with health and psychological
well-being. If friends do not encourage each
other to comply with diet, exercise, medication,
or other health-related recommendations, if they
offer unsolicited and unhelpful advice, if they
are self-centered and cannot reciprocate social
and emotional support, if they betray confiden-
ces or trust, or if they do not convey caring and

understanding, then they are likely to foster
psychological distress and diminish health
rather than providing the kind of support that
sustains general well-being (Gallant, Spitze, and
Prohaska, 2007; Roberto and Husser, 2007).
Implications for Professional Practice

Given the many extensive benefits of friendship,
it is important for professionals to consider the
variety of ways friends might be tapped to sup-
plement or, in some cases, substitute for aid
and emotional sustenance typically provided by
family members. Although friends may limit the
extent to which they will agree to carry out very
personal body care and intensive, ongoing forms
of assistance near the end of life (Messeri, Sil-
verstein, and Litwak, 1993), they can certainly
continue to provide social and emotional
support and companionship for even the frail-
est individuals. Moreover, for those elders, and
for persons who are less frail but still require
supervision and assistance because of conditions
such as rehabilitation from medical procedures
or early memory loss, friends can provide tem-
porary respite for family and professional
caregivers who assume the majority of the more
involved care and support.
So it is worth educating older adults about

the value of continuing friendship interactions.
Friends should not think they will get in the
way of family and health professionals. Some-
times people feel they should stay away from
their friends in need, not because they don’t
care, but because they don’t know how to
proceed. Likewise, it may be necessary to
encourage family and health professionals to

Research has shown the promise of direct
interventions to promote social integration and
mitigate loneliness and isolation due to lack of
social skills, social withdrawal, or poor health
(Adams and Blieszner, 1993). These programs
could be replicated across many communities
to extend the benefits to other older adults. For
example, Stevens, Martina, and Westerhof (2006)
explored the effects of a program designed to
educate lonely older women about effective ways
to develop new friendships and improve existing
ones to help reduce loneliness. Twelve weekly
lessons on self-esteem, relational competence,
friendship formation and maintenance, and
social skills were offered to a treatment group;
both treatment and control group members were
assessed on loneliness and friendship informa-

tion before and after the intervention.
The results showed that compared to the
control group, more of the women who received
guidance on friendship formation and mainte-
nance had made new friends and more had
improved existing friendships at follow-up.
Moreover, those who both made new friends
and improved existing friend ties reported less
loneliness. Women in the treatment group
displayed greater increases in taking initiative
and a greater tendency to invest in friendship
(Martina, Stevens, and Westerhof, 2012), show-
ing they had mastered key friendship skills. The
