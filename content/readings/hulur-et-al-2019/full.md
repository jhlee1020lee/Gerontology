## Abstract

Studies of historical and societal influences on cognitive aging generally show that later-born cohorts outperform earlier-born cohorts on tests of fluid cognitive performance. Those historical gains are often linked to improvements in education during childhood and adolescence, but much less is known about the role of adult work environments. Over the last century, work demands and work organization changed profoundly as many societies shifted from manufacturing to service and technical economies.

Using data from the Seattle Longitudinal Study, the authors compared age-related trajectories of change in five primary mental abilities between earlier-born cohorts (1901-1938) and later-born cohorts (1939-1966). Cohorts were matched observation by observation on age and retest, and the analyses controlled for gender and number of observations. Later-born cohorts performed better on most cognitive tasks and showed less age-related decline in word fluency. They also reported more enriched work environments, especially higher worker control and innovation, though not higher work autonomy. More enriched work environments were associated with higher cognitive performance at age 55, these effects were independent of education, and they were largely consistent across cohorts. The authors conclude that perceived work environment is associated with cognitive functioning independently of education and across historical time.

## Introduction

Later-born cohorts have repeatedly been shown to outperform earlier-born cohorts on tests of fluid cognitive performance at the same age. This pattern, closely related to the Flynn effect, has been documented not only in adolescence and early adulthood but well into old age. What remains less clear is whether later-born cohorts also age more favorably, that is, whether they decline more slowly as they get older. Previous studies point in different directions: some report shallower age-related decline for later-born cohorts, some find steeper decline, and others find no reliable cohort differences in the rate of change.

The usual explanation for later-born cohorts' higher performance emphasizes education. Both years of schooling and educational quality are associated with late-life cognitive levels and trajectories, and both increased substantially across the twentieth century. Yet adulthood also changed historically. Work became less centered on routinized manual labor and more centered on service, technical, and professional activity. If adult cognition is shaped not only by schooling but also by the kinds of demands people face at work, then historical changes in work may help explain cohort differences in cognitive aging as well.

## Cohort Differences in Cognitive Aging: Possible Explanations

Earlier work on cohort differences in cognition proposed several historical explanations: better education, smaller families, changing parental attitudes toward schooling, and improvements in nutrition and hygiene. Those explanations mainly concern childhood and adolescence. From a life-span developmental perspective, however, adult developmental tasks matter too, and work is one of the central tasks of adulthood.

During the twentieth century, the structure of work changed in ways that may matter cognitively. Postindustrial economies increased the share of professional, technical, and service jobs. Work also changed within occupations, not just across them. Jobs increasingly allowed more decision making by workers and relied less on simple routinized execution. These shifts raise the possibility that later-born cohorts encountered work environments that were more cognitively enriching than those experienced by earlier-born cohorts.

## Work Environment and Cognitive Aging

The article builds on the "use it or lose it" hypothesis, according to which cognitively engaging environments help maintain higher levels of functioning in later life. Applied to work, the idea is that jobs requiring self-direction, active problem solving, novelty, and control may support cognitive development and maintenance, whereas simple and routinized work may do the opposite.

Prior research generally shows that more stimulating work is associated with higher cognitive performance across adulthood. Complex and innovative work has also been linked to healthier brain aging. The evidence is less consistent for age-related change: some studies find slower decline for those with more stimulating work histories, while others find effects only for some abilities. Even so, the broader historical shift toward more complex and technical work suggests that work environment is a plausible contributor to cohort differences in adult cognitive functioning.

## The Present Study

The authors use longitudinal data from the Seattle Longitudinal Study (SLS) to examine whether differences in perceived work environment and education help explain cohort differences in cognitive aging. They compare earlier-born participants (1901-1938) and later-born participants (1939-1966) across the same age range, 21 to 73. Matching was done on age and retest at the observation level, not simply at the person level, and the models also accounted for gender and number of observations.

The study focuses on three work-environment dimensions: work control, work autonomy, and work innovation. Work control refers to the extent to which employees control the work process rather than following externally imposed rules. Work autonomy refers to the extent to which workers make their own decisions and act independently. Work innovation refers to the extent to which work involves variety, change, and new approaches. The expectation was that later-born cohorts would show higher cognitive performance, and that more cognitively enriching work environments would be associated with higher cognitive levels and more favorable age trajectories independently of education.

## Method

### Participants and Procedure

The SLS began in 1956 and followed community-dwelling adults in the Seattle metropolitan area across repeated seven-year waves. For the present study, the relevant data came from nine waves of cognitive testing between 1956 and 2012, while work-environment data were available in 1991, 1998, 2005, and 2012. The analytic sample included 2,362 participants, 53% women, who had at least one cognitive assessment plus data on work environment and education. Their average age at first available wave was 50 years, with a range from 21 to 84.

Participants contributed up to nine longitudinal observations over as many as 56 years. On average, they provided 3.24 observations. The earlier-born cohort contributed more observations on average than the later-born cohort, which is one reason the authors used matching procedures before estimating cohort differences.

### Measures

Five cognitive abilities were examined using Thurstone's Primary Mental Abilities tests, all converted to T-score metrics using the first SLS occasion as reference. Spatial orientation measured the ability to judge object rotation in two-dimensional space. Inductive reasoning measured the ability to infer the rule governing letter series. Word fluency measured how many valid words participants could generate beginning with the letter S within five minutes. Number ability measured simple addition skill by comparing correct and incorrect arithmetic responses. Verbal meaning measured the ability to identify the correct synonym for a target word. One-month test-retest reliability for these measures was reported as high.

Cohort was coded as a binary indicator: those born in 1938 or earlier were classified as pre-World War II, and those born in 1939 or later were classified as post-World War II. Chronological age was used as the within-person time metric for modeling cognitive change.

### Perceived Work Environment

Beginning in 1991, participants completed an abbreviated 15-item Work Environment Scale. Depending on their status, they reported on their current workplace, their main job, their last major job if retired, or homemaking conditions if they were full-time homemakers. The Control subscale captured how much management relied on rules and pressure, reverse-scored so that higher values reflected more worker control. The Autonomy subscale captured how much workers could make their own decisions and be self-sufficient. The Innovation subscale captured the degree to which work involved variety, change, and new approaches.

Because participants had up to four work-environment measurements, the authors aggregated them to characterize perceived work environment as broadly as possible. If a participant had only one wave, that score was used. If a participant had multiple waves and was working in at least one of them, the score was averaged across working waves. For the remaining participants, ratings were averaged across retirement or homemaking reports. In the final sample, 59% of the ratings referred to current work, 36% to a job from which the participant had retired, and 6% to homemaking.

### Covariates

Gender was coded as men versus women. Education was measured as years of formal schooling, using the highest reported value when more than one measure was available. Retest was coded as 0 for the first cognitive assessment and 1 for all later assessments to separate aging from familiarity with the tests. The models also controlled for the number of longitudinal observations available per participant after matching.

### Data Preparation

The first step in data preparation was observation-level propensity score matching. Participants in the earlier-born cohort had more opportunities to contribute observations because they entered the study in earlier waves. To reduce that imbalance, the authors matched each later-born observation against an earlier-born observation with the same or very similar age and retest status. Starting from 2,942 later-born observations and 4,714 earlier-born observations, the matching procedure retained 1,711 observations in each cohort using a caliper of 0.06 standard deviations. After matching, cohort differences in age and retest were no longer statistically reliable.

The second step was to include additional covariates in the growth models: gender, education, retest, and number of observations. The authors note that they could have placed more variables directly into the propensity score, but that would have sharply reduced the number of acceptable matches. Age was centered at 55 years, other covariates were centered at their sample means, and continuous predictors such as education and work-environment scores were z-standardized. Later-born cohorts had higher work-control and work-innovation scores, but not higher work-autonomy scores.

### Data Analysis

The main analyses used growth models to estimate age-related change in each cognitive ability. At Level 1, the models represented each person's cognitive score as a function of age, age squared, retest, and residual error. This allowed the authors to estimate both linear and accelerated change with age while separating those effects from repeated-test familiarity.

At Level 2, the person-specific intercept and slope terms were predicted by cohort, one work-environment score at a time, gender, education, number of observations, and the interaction between cohort and work environment. Separate models were run for work control, work autonomy, and work innovation. The intercept represented cognitive level at age 55, and the slope parameters represented change with age.

## Results

### Cohort Differences in Cognitive Functioning

Across all five cognitive tests, the matched sample performed above the original 1956 SLS reference mean at age 55. Performance declined with age in every domain, and the quadratic age terms showed that decline accelerated at older ages. The central cohort finding was that later-born participants scored higher at age 55 on spatial orientation, inductive reasoning, word fluency, and verbal meaning, but lower on number ability.

Later-born participants also showed less steep age-related decline in inductive reasoning, word fluency, and verbal meaning. There were no reliable cohort differences in the rate of change for spatial orientation or number ability. In other words, cohort differences in cognitive level were broad and consistent, whereas cohort differences in cognitive change were more selective.

### The Role of Perceived Work Environment

When work-environment variables and covariates were added, each work dimension showed a somewhat different pattern. Greater work control was associated with higher levels of performance in all five cognitive tests, and with less age-related decline in inductive reasoning. Greater work autonomy was associated with higher levels of performance in all tests except spatial orientation, and with less decline in spatial orientation and inductive reasoning. Greater work innovation was associated with higher levels of performance in spatial orientation and verbal meaning, but not in inductive reasoning, word fluency, or number ability.

Most of these work-environment effects were consistent across cohorts. The only reliable moderation was for number ability: the positive association between work autonomy and number ability at age 55 was weaker in the later-born cohort. After controlling for work environment, gender, and education, cohort differences in level remained reliable for spatial orientation, inductive reasoning, and number ability. For word fluency and verbal meaning, however, cohort differences in level were no longer reliable once work environment and covariates were included.

The authors also ran three follow-up checks. First, they excluded participants who reported homemaking at every occasion, and the findings remained the same. Second, they repeated analyses with and without the retest variable, and the work-environment effects were unchanged. Third, they added household income, standardized within wave and averaged across 1991 to 2012. Income predicted higher levels of most cognitive outcomes and less decline in inductive reasoning, but it did not eliminate the effects of perceived work environment.

## Discussion

The study asked whether perceived work environment helps explain cohort differences in age-related cognitive performance. The answer was broadly yes. Later-born cohorts had more cognitively enriching work environments in some respects, especially work control and innovation, and these more enriched work environments were associated with higher cognitive performance independently of education. At the same time, cohort differences in cognition were not reduced to work alone, because some cohort effects remained even after work variables and covariates were considered.

### Cohort Differences in Cognitive Performance

The finding that later-born cohorts outperformed earlier-born cohorts on most cognitive abilities matches prior work from the Seattle Longitudinal Study and other cohort studies. The main exception was number ability, where the later-born cohort performed worse. The authors interpret that pattern as consistent with historical shifts away from rote memorization and toward reliance on calculators and related devices.

The findings on rates of age-related change were more mixed. Later-born cohorts declined less in inductive reasoning, word fluency, and verbal meaning, but not in spatial orientation or number ability. The authors note that this partly differs from earlier SLS work, likely because the current study used a different cohort split and focused on a somewhat different age range.

### The Role of History-Graded Differences in Perceived Work Environment

Later-born cohorts reported higher work control and higher work innovation, but not higher work autonomy. The authors connect this pattern to a broader literature on historical change in control beliefs. Later-born cohorts appear to report lower external control without necessarily reporting higher internal control, which parallels the distinction between work control and work autonomy used here.

The cognitive correlates of work were substantial. Higher work control and work autonomy related to better performance on almost every measure examined. Work innovation related to some abilities, especially spatial orientation and verbal meaning. Work control and autonomy also related to less decline in inductive reasoning, and autonomy additionally related to less decline in spatial orientation. Importantly, these associations held even after controlling for education, suggesting that adult work experiences matter over and above early-life educational advantage.

The authors are careful not to claim a one-way causal pathway. More enriching work may help sustain cognition, but it is also plausible that people with higher cognitive functioning are more likely to select into jobs with greater control, autonomy, and innovation. Even with that caution, the findings still support the broader idea that historically changing work environments are one meaningful context for understanding historically changing cognition.

### Limitations and Outlook

The article notes several limitations. Perceived work environment was measured less often than cognition, so it was treated as a time-invariant predictor. The study also could not estimate how long participants spent in a particular job or work setting. Both limitations matter because the effects of work may depend on duration and on within-person change in work conditions over time.

Work environment was measured subjectively, often retrospectively for retired participants. Occupational databases might have provided a more objective coding scheme, but the authors argue that subjective perception is important in its own right and that the same job title may have involved different demands in different historical periods. Another concern is that the work-environment subscales were correlated with one another, so they may partly reflect a broader general factor of work enrichment rather than sharply distinct mechanisms.

The sample was drawn from the upper three quarters of the socioeconomic spectrum and was positively selected in education, so the effects may be even stronger in more heterogeneous samples. The age range was also limited to 21 through 73 to preserve comparability across cohorts. Since earlier work suggests that cohort differences may narrow at very old ages and near death, future research should examine whether work remains linked to cognition long after retirement and into later phases of aging.

### Conclusions

The study shows that later-born cohorts experienced more cognitively enriching work environments in some respects, and that those work environments were linked to higher cognitive performance independently of education. Work control, work autonomy, and work innovation did not operate identically, but together they help explain why cognition changes historically across cohorts. The broader implication is that adult work life is not just a social or economic context; it is also part of the developmental environment that shapes how cognition is maintained across the adult life course.
