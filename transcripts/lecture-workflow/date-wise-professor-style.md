## 2026-03-05 `ch01-gerontology`

### Preferred Answer Patterns

- Do not stop at saying something was interesting; explain what was new and why it matters.
- Distinguish close terms precisely and include the English label when relevant.
- When giving historical or institutional background, name the concrete actor and context instead of using broad labels.
- Explain age categories as socially constructed criteria, not as self-evident natural facts.

### Likely Follow-Up Questions

- Why did age 65 become the old-age threshold? Was there a scientific basis for it?
- What is the difference between gerontology and geriatrics?
- What exactly do you mean by "the West"? Can you be more specific?
- What time span do you mean by "not that long ago," and why was that surprising?

## 2026-03-10 `ch02-gerontology`

### Preferred Answer Patterns

- Move from a big-picture answer to the mechanism that produces the phenomenon.
- For concept questions, explain the structure of the indicator, not just the label.
- If something was memorable, explain what prior assumption it changed.
- When challenged, restate the concept in your own words rather than waiting for the professor to finish the answer.

### Likely Follow-Up Questions

- Why do you think that happened? Can you say it in a more precise way?
- What concept are you talking about? Say it again.
- Conceptually, what does the old-age dependency ratio mean?
- What goes in the numerator and the denominator?
- Why was age 15 used as the productive-population cutoff, and why does that fit less well now?

## 2026-03-12 `beck-2016`

### Preferred Answer Patterns

- Answer the exact question that was asked; do not drift into a nearby topic.
- Strong answers and essays include personal examples, honest self-positioning, and a clear insight.
- When you mention a role or effect, specify who is judging whom and how it operates.
- Use your own language rather than polished summary language that sounds AI-generated.

### Likely Follow-Up Questions

- Why did you come to think that way?
- What do you personally think about it?
- What do you mean specifically?
- Who is evaluating whom in that example?
- Listen to the question again and answer that question directly.

## 2026-03-17 `ch03-gerontology`

### Preferred Answer Patterns

- Interview-based answers should preserve concrete life history and historical context.
- Explain why the interview target is likely to speak openly and meaningfully.
- Keep vivid wording or dialect when it carries the life of the interview material.
- Interpret the narrative through life-course themes instead of leaving it as a raw story.

### Likely Follow-Up Questions

- Has enough rapport been formed for that interview to work?
- Why are you choosing that person as the interview target?
- Where do you see time and place, timing, linked lives, or human agency in that case?
- How did that historical event shape the person?s life course?
- Do not stop at the narrative itself; explain how the theory is embedded in the life story.

## 2026-03-19 `ch04-gerontology`

### Preferred Answer Patterns

- State the research design in both Korean and English, then attach one concrete example.
- Do not recite theories or methods as a list; show how they work in a real case.
- When discussing the interview assignment, justify target choice in terms of likely story depth and access.
- Preserve vivid expressions when they strengthen the realism of the written account.

### Likely Follow-Up Questions

- What is a cross-sectional design?
- What is a longitudinal design, and how is it different from cross-sectional?
- If you turned that idea into a hypothesis, what design would you use?
- Why do you think that interview target will produce a genuinely interesting account?
- Where exactly is the theory visible in that person?s life?

## 2026-03-24 `hulur-et-al-2019`

### Preferred Answer Patterns

- To say you read the article, you must explain the study design and its implementation, not just the abstract or conclusion.
- If you call a finding interesting or new, explain why it was new and what you had expected before reading it.
- Use exact names for effects, designs, and variables, and add the English term when needed.
- Do not stop at one explanation; compare plausible alternative explanations.
- Prepare both Korean and English key terms and the exact paper variables for quiz-style or oral follow-up questions.

### Likely Follow-Up Questions

- Did you actually read it? What idea did you get from the part you read?
- Why is that new?
- What effect is that called?
- Why is it called the Flynn effect?
- Why would formal education be advantageous on IQ tests?
- What occupational environment characteristics did the Hulur paper actually examine?
